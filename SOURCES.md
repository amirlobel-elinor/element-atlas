# Chemistry references and conventions

Reviewed 21 October 2026. The app's question wording, explanations, design, and illustrations are original. Element names and initial numeric references were retained from the supplied app, with the changes and limits below. External sources inform the chemistry; no scraped flashcard sets or copied textbook question banks are included.

## Deliberate teaching choices

**Atomic weights.** Displayed values are approximate classroom references, not a complete uncertainty-qualified reproduction of CIAAW's table. Argon is displayed as 39.95, zirconium as 91.222, gadolinium as 157.249, and lutetium as 174.96669. Rounding precision varies because the original app's other numeric references were retained. For graded coursework, use the values supplied by your instructor. Weight quizzes accept an absolute difference of at most 0.5 and do not test elements without a standard atomic weight.

**Isotope brackets.** Where no standard atomic weight is available, brackets surround a selected isotope's mass number from the original dataset. This is not an average atomic weight, not a neutron count, and not a claim that the isotope is necessarily the current longest-lived choice. Thorium, protactinium, and uranium have standard atomic weights and are not bracketed.

**Groups and categories.** The compact table uses 18 columns, with La–Lu and Ac–Lr detached. This avoids assigning a group-3 answer to a disputed choice within those series. Category boundaries are conventional classroom choices, not universal standards: group 12 is grouped visually with transition metals; polonium is shown as post-transition; the six metalloids are B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, and Te. Main-group family names and broad material categories are different classifications. Hydrogen is excluded from family quizzes rather than being labeled an alkali metal.

**Valence.** Practice uses the familiar neutral main-group count through radon, including helium's two electrons. Transition metals, detached series, and superheavy elements are excluded. A missing value in this deck is not a claim that valence electrons are undefined.

**Ions.** The dataset lists selected common monatomic ions for introductory chemistry. It is deliberately not an exhaustive oxidation-state list. For example, a high positive oxidation state in an oxoanion is not presented as a common free monatomic ion. Mercury(I) is not treated as Hg⁺; the usual mercury(I) ion is Hg₂²⁺, so mercury is omitted from this simple-ion deck. Hydrogen's context-dependent ion chemistry is also omitted.

**Trends.** Comparisons use 18 selected pairs that follow broad atomic-radius or first-ionization-energy trends. Explanations use “generally” because trends have exceptions. The deck does not make arbitrary pair comparisons or quiz minor exceptions as if they were predictable from the simple trend.

**Concepts.** The 20 reasoning questions connect atomic number, electron count, isotope mass number, charge balance, nomenclature, and periodic structure. The 12 polyatomic-ion questions use formulas and charges, including parentheses in compound-formula examples. These banks are intentionally finite and can be expanded in `data.js`.

**Superheavy elements.** Locations and names are included for all 118 elements. Category placement does not establish a superheavy element's observed state or chemical behavior; many properties remain predicted or uncertain.
